Asymmetric Links SNR direction gap, largest first

SourcePeer SNRRev SNR Within-row ΔCross-dir ΔMax Δ

What this shows. An RF link should be roughly equal in both directions. This section lists links where the two directions disagree on signal quality (largest gap first). Δ values are in dB; a gap of 3–6 dB is marginal and >6 dB is poor.

  • SNR — the signal-to-noise ratio the source node hears from the peer (how well we receive them).
  • Rev SNR — the reverse direction: the SNR the peer hears from the source (how well they receive us), as reported in the source's LQM.
  • Within-row Δ — |SNR − Rev SNR| from one node's own LQM (the two directions of the link as that node sees them).
  • Cross-dir Δ — the gap between each node's own received SNR of the other, available only when both ends were polled (an independent cross-check).
  • Max Δ — the larger of the two; the headline asymmetry used for ranking.

The problem & likely causes. A large gap means one direction is much weaker — classically "we hear them fine but they can't hear us" (or vice-versa). Common causes: mismatched transmit power between the ends, different antenna gain or aiming, a one-sided obstruction or interference source, a degraded radio/feedline on one end, or a hidden-node / near-far situation.

Potential fixes. Re-aim or upgrade the antenna on the weaker end; equalize TX power between the two radios; clear or work around an obstruction and hunt one-sided interference (check the noise floor on the weak side); verify the distance setting and both radios' health. If it persists, reposition the weaker end.

Impact on throughput & latency. A radio link is only as good as its worse direction: the modulation/data rate is negotiated down to what the weak path supports, driving retransmits, higher latency and jitter, and lower throughput. Because TCP ACKs travel the reverse path, a weak return direction caps end-to-end TCP speed even if the forward path is strong. Severe asymmetry also makes LQM penalize or block the link, which shows up as flapping — and it degrades VOIP into one-way audio or choppy calls.

How uptime is measured

Up means running and answering. Time next to a failed poll counts as down (even if the node kept running but couldn't be reached), and so does the time before a reboot. Each answered poll reports how long the node has been running, which bridges gaps where nobody polled it. Long gaps where nobody polled the node and its uptime doesn't cover them are unchecked and left out, as is time in a maintenance window. Uptime % = up / (up + down). Nodes whose failed polls are explained (tunnel-only, answers ping but not HTTP, placeholder) show n/a: the monitor can't tell. A node is judged up to its last poll; "stale" means it hasn't been polled lately.

How it is estimated. Capacity is the median of the overnight scheduled runs (quiet network) over the last 7 nights, or the 90th percentile of all runs until there are some. Key links are the RF links with an infrastructure end, both directions, plus any you add. Traffic comes from the radios' packet counters at an assumed 600-byte frame (AREDN does not report channel busy), skipping intervals with an iPerf test, so treat spare capacity as a rough figure.
